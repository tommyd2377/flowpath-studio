**FlowPath Studio: product experience and voice/visual acceptance research**

September 12, 2026. Foundation for the [GTM and growth plan](/Users/thomasdevito/Projects/theVoice/docs/gtm-growth-strategy-2026-09.md). This combines direct code and local UI inspection with public research. It contains no original customer-study results. Proposed tests and thresholds are explicitly hypotheses.

**The product has two customers whose acceptance must be earned separately.**

The publisher uses Studio to supply knowledge, identity and approved capabilities, review interpretations, correct facts, preview the result and publish. The visitor uses the resulting application to express an intention and work with information on screen. The publisher buys an experience delivered under their own brand; the visitor supplies attention, trust and sometimes microphone access. Both determine whether the subscription lasts.

Single-user value exists on both sides: a publisher benefits without recruiting other publishers, and a visitor benefits without other visitors joining. This is a publishing platform with two linked user journeys. It does not need marketplace liquidity to become useful. Visitors matter as customers of the experience even when the publisher pays the platform bill.

**What the product actually looks and behaves like.**

I read the seven requested documents, the launch offer and the adaptive-workspace assignment; traced Studio, rendering, voice, screen-context and publication code; and used the actual application in an isolated local demo. The review used the repository's fictional workshop CSV, demo providers and separate temporary storage. No production publication, microphone permission or paid model call was used.

The voice implementation routes navigation and presentation into the same rendering system used by manual interaction. Screen context captures actual viewport, visible entities, controls and focus; navigation checks whether its target was applied. That is the foundation for language such as “that one.” The broad promise of manipulating several persistent, independently addressable objects still needs integrated verification. [Screen context and navigation](/Users/thomasdevito/Projects/theVoice/lib/visitor-view-context.ts:32), [interaction state](/Users/thomasdevito/Projects/theVoice/packages/platform-ui/src/interaction.ts:13).

There were two practical verification limits. The in-app browser had no installed device voice for the offline preview, so it displayed its typing fallback; this does not establish anything about production speech quality. On the mobile focused card, a pointer attempt at Back to results hit the waveform area; the return target's observed top position overlapped that area. Keyboard activation returned to comparison. Treat this as a concrete mobile interaction issue to reproduce and resolve before launch, not a quantified failure rate. Existing mocked voice tests were inspected but not run as a fresh suite here. Natural conversation, acoustic conditions, real-device behavior and visitor comfort remain unverified. The local server was stopped after inspection.

**What existing research supports—and its limits.**

I did not find representative evidence that directly estimates willingness to converse with a waveform on an unfamiliar business website that rearranges on-screen objects. Smart-speaker ownership, chatbot adoption, reactions to customer-service automation and preference in a laboratory task measure different things. None supplies a credible percentage for FlowPath's adoption forecast.

| Evidence | Finding relevant to FlowPath | What it cannot establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chen et al., 2023: within-subject deployment, 16 independently living older adults, ages 70–97, over 40 days | Participants valued visual output while preferring speech responses over touch. This supports testing speech input plus a useful screen, including with older users. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.07723) | Small, specific home-device sample; it does not show older adults generally prefer voice websites or predict purchasing behavior. |
| Saktheeswaran et al., 2020: qualitative comparison of touch, speech and their combination for visual network exploration | Participants favored multimodal input for freedom of expression and complementary, integrated interaction. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.14505) | A specialist visualization task is not consumer browsing; the study does not supply a general market-adoption rate. |
| Ge et al., CHI 2025: GenComUI, within-subject study with 20 participants, compared with voice-only robot task programming | Nineteen preferred the visual-aid condition; median SUS was 73.75 versus 63.75. Crucially, the detailed results found no significant difference in completion time or dialogue turns; everyone completed tasks in both systems. [Full paper, results and limitations](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.10678v1) | Improved perceived usability does not prove faster completion. The controlled, mostly university sample and spatial robot tasks do not validate consumer websites or superiority over conventional browsing. |
| Thankachan et al., March 2026: seven focus groups, 22 university-community participants ages 21–42, discussing design-fiction scenarios | Views on public voice use were mixed; comfort depended on setting and what information would be spoken. Some public situations were acceptable, while sensitive disclosure remained concerning. [Study](https://www.mdpi.com/2414-4088/10/3/31) | Participants discussed hypothetical ideal scenarios. These are useful objections and context hypotheses, not observed adoption or evidence that all public voice use fails. |
| Gartner, July 2024, reporting a December 2023 survey of 5,728 customers | Sixty-four percent preferred companies not use AI for customer service; difficulty reaching a human was a major concern. Publishers may associate an AI interface with this kind of customer frustration. [Survey release](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2024-07-09-gartner-survey-finds-64-percent-of-customers-would-prefer-that-companies-didnt-use-ai-for-customer-service) | This older service-automation survey does not mean 64% would reject FlowPath. Voluntary browsing assistance and blocked access to a person are different experiences. |
| Nielsen Norman Group, 2017 design analysis | Speech can express intent efficiently; a screen supports parallel inspection and reduces memory demands. Integrated voice/screen interaction and discoverable commands matter. [Voice and screen analysis](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/voice-first/), [interaction signifiers](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/audio-signifiers-voice-interaction/) | These are durable design arguments, not current model-performance measurements or a modern preference survey. |

My interpretation is that hybrid interaction has a credible usability rationale, with promising but indirect empirical support. It remains an unproven business proposition for this exact product. The strongest hypothesis is that visitors can state a complex need, see the system's interpretation, inspect the resulting objects and correct it directly. The novelty becomes useful when it reduces effort or improves understanding while preserving control.

**Separate speech input, spoken output, visual change and entry design.**

“Do you like voice?” combines four independent choices. A person may enjoy speaking a complex request, prefer a silent visual answer, dislike an unsolicited spoken greeting, and still want to tap an item. Another may need spoken output and rarely look at the screen. Treat these as interaction choices within one task rather than permanent demographic personas.

| Context / visitor job | Hybrid opportunity to test | Likely friction to investigate | Proposed default to test |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mobile at home, exploring an unfamiliar offer | Speak several constraints without opening a keyboard; inspect options and refine them | Unclear listening state, long responses, permission hesitation | Prominent waveform, a specific example request, visible Type and Browse; initiate microphone only after an intentional action |
| Mobile in a quiet/shared setting | Continue the same exploration through typing and touch | Overhearing, social discomfort, unwanted audio | Easy silent path; preserve the current task when changing input/output mode |
| Desktop in private, comparing several options | Keep two items visible; point/click and refer to “this one”; speech handles complex filtering | Screen movement disrupts scanning or overwrites useful information | Stable objects, clear selection, brief speech, reversible changes; retain mouse and keyboard precision |
| Desktop in a shared workplace | Typed intent can still assemble the same useful objects | Speaking may interrupt others; confidential query content | Immediate typing and manual controls, with optional speech when appropriate |
| Fast factual visit: hours, location or contact | Immediate answer with an appropriate card | Starting a conversation is more effort than reading one line | Essential information reachable without a dialogue; do not make conversation length a success metric |
| Visual, motor, hearing or speech access needs | Different combinations of speech, text, keyboard and screen-reader output may help | A preferred modality for one person can exclude another | Accessible alternative routes and participant-specific testing; never infer universal accessibility from the presence of voice |

These are proposed tests, not measured preference findings. Mobile does not always mean public, and desktop does not always mean private. Headphones reduce audible output but do not make the visitor's own speech private. Do not infer situational context or accessibility needs from device type alone.

The waveform should communicate a usable state: ready, listening, processing, speaking or stopped. It also needs a clear first action and a understandable scope. Proposed copy: “Tell me what you're looking for. I'll show you the options.” Follow with a concrete, published-content example and visible alternatives. Do not imply an actual person or uninterrupted listening. Any claim about recording, retention or microphone state must match the implementation.

For the intended object workspace, test: “Show beginner classes” → display a few cards → tap one → “Compare this with the drawing class” → display a comparison → “Keep those and show the location” → retain the comparison and reveal location → “Close the location” → restore the earlier arrangement. The last two steps require the planned independent-object model. Use a clearly labeled prototype until those behaviors work. Visual acknowledgment should reflect an applied action; ambiguity should produce a small choice, not a confident change to the wrong object.

**Test publisher confidence with their visitors, not with a generic demo alone.**

Before showing the product, ask each owner for a recent customer-selection problem, its frequency, their existing solution, the audience's circumstances, and the commercial consequence. Then show Studio and the visitor experience separately. Ask the owner to predict how many of ten of their visitors would understand the opening, voluntarily try speech and complete a task. Record the prediction before presenting visitor results.

Recruit actual or closely representative visitors from that business, including voice skeptics and people who ordinarily browse silently. After observing them, ask the owner to update their prediction, choose a real deployment placement and decide whether to start paying when the offer is available. A hypothetical “I like it” has substantially less weight than allocating traffic, correcting their own content and making a real commercial decision.

| Publisher concern to investigate | Concrete evidence or experience that addresses it |
| --- | --- |
| “My customers won't talk to a website.” | Report voluntary choice and task success by context, including successful silent visits. Show how the same task continues across input methods. |
| “It may say the wrong thing under my brand.” | Preview representative and deliberately unanswerable questions; show sources privately, corrections, publication boundaries and clear uncertainty. |
| “It feels impressive but will lose enquiries.” | Compare the current journey with the hybrid experience using equal content and traffic eligibility; count downstream outcomes separately from clicks. |
| “I can't maintain another website.” | Measure a second source update, correction persistence and owner intervention minutes. |
| “Will I pay for people experimenting with the voice?” | Explain bounded usage and show actual direct cost per useful session; keep visitors' browsing functional when the allowance ends. |

These are hypotheses to investigate; no publisher interviews were conducted for this report.

**A six-week acceptance study before selecting the launch default.**

1. **Week 1: establish the baseline and recruit.** Define three tasks using the same approved content: a simple fact, a multi-constraint choice and a visual/reference task. Recruit 12 publishers across two candidate segments and 24 end visitors for formative testing. Include regular and infrequent voice users, private/shared usage circumstances, varied ages and accents, and participants with relevant access needs. This sample discovers problems; it cannot estimate population preference by subgroup.
2. **Weeks 2–3: compare interaction methods.** Use A: conventional visual browsing; B: typed intent plus adaptive objects; C: speech plus those same objects with touch/keyboard available. Counterbalance task/order with equivalent scenarios so users do not merely repeat an answer. Observe the first task without explaining the waveform. In the remaining tasks, distinguish assigned-mode performance from a free-choice task. Keep source content, task difficulty and output quality as comparable as possible. Measure the extra value of speech by C versus B, and adaptive composition by B versus A.
3. **Week 4: test the opening separately.** Compare the present waveform-dominant opening with waveform plus visible Type/Browse and a specific example. Use fresh participants where possible. Do not attribute an entry-design effect to speech quality. Test assistant-first versus visitor-first and spoken versus silent output later, one material factor at a time.
4. **Weeks 5–6: observe use with less prompting.** Invite an initial consenting cohort to use the experience for naturally occurring tasks over approximately two weeks. Capture a genuine new need separately from a scheduled test. Ask the publishers to compare predictions with observed results and choose whether to direct a bounded share of traffic to the pilot. Paid live-provider evaluation is a separate authorized test activity; the local demo cannot substitute for natural speech performance.

Use moderated observation for mechanisms, then a powered field comparison for commercial claims. Compensation and reminders can increase return rates; disclose both. Do not market a study's prompted speech usage as voluntary adoption. Do not discard permission refusal, abandonment or failed interpretation from the visitor denominator.

**Measures and predeclared decisions.**

| Measure | Definition | Initial proposed acceptance gate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First-use comprehension | Can explain how to start and what will happen within ten seconds, without coaching | Aim ≥80% in formative sessions; report counts and uncertainty |
| Unassisted task completion | Correct answer/choice under a predefined rubric; abandoned tasks count as failures | Aim ≥85% for supported tasks before an external pilot; no known critical factual/action errors |
| Voluntary voice use | Visitors choosing speech / all eligible exposed visitors; also show technically available denominator | Establish baseline by task and context; no universal minimum share |
| Permission funnel | Wave tap → prompt, grant/deny, ready, first actual utterance, first useful visible result | Report each loss separately; do not interpret denied access as a model failure |
| Reference resolution | Correct intended object / all attempted references, including “that,” relative position and previously opened objects | Aim ≥95% on a bounded authored acceptance set; unknown/ambiguous references must recover usefully |
| Recovery and control | Failed request or wrong target → successful correction or manual completion; ability to stop speech and restore view | Aim ≥90% successful recovery on the acceptance set; inspect every control trap |
| Response experience | End of speech → first meaningful visual result; end of speech → first audible response; interruption → silence | Proposed p50 visual ≤2 seconds, p95 ≤5; interruption p95 ≤500 ms. Validate on real devices/networks; do not label filler speech as task progress |
| Trust and effort | Post-task ease rating, confidence in displayed facts, observed hesitation and correction burden | No material deterioration from the visual baseline; investigate overconfidence as well as distrust |
| Reuse on a new need | Successful return among visitors who had a new relevant task during the observation window | Establish a task-specific baseline. A completed one-time visit is not a retention failure |
| Publisher commitment | Owner supplies sources, approves content, gives traffic placement and accepts an actual paid start | Seek five of 12 qualified pilots; publish reasons for rejection and changes after seeing visitor evidence |

These numerical gates are product choices, not industry benchmarks, and small samples do not statistically certify them. For a field comparison, predeclare acceptable harm and useful benefit—for example, reject a launch default that lowers correct completion by more than five percentage points; seek +10 points completion or a clearly valued effort reduction without lost correctness. Estimating a small non-inferiority margin needs substantially more data than a formative study. About 387 independent observations per arm are needed for a conventional 40%→50% test at 5% two-sided significance and 80% power, before clustering, attrition and multiplicity adjustments.

Decision rules: if hybrid use improves the relevant task and owners commit traffic/payment, proceed in that context. If typed adaptive interaction works while speech is rarely chosen, retain the hybrid architecture and revise the voice entry/distribution hypothesis. If speech is valued in private but avoided in public, serve both contexts without forcing one mode. If owners remain unwilling after visitors succeed, test brand-control, maintenance and economic objections. If neither visitor outcomes nor owner maintenance improve enough to justify payment, change the job or segment before scaling acquisition.
